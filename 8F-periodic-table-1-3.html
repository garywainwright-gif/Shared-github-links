The modern periodic table

A group is a vertical column. Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties — they react in similar ways. Group 1 (alkali metals) is on the far left. Group 7 (halogens) and Group 0 (noble gases) are on the far right.

A period is a horizontal row. As you move left to right across a period, the elements change gradually from metals towards non-metals.

1. Atoms, elements and Dalton’s model

Must know

An atom is the smallest particle of an element that still has the properties of that element. You cannot see atoms with a school microscope — they are far too small. Models (drawings and ideas) help us talk about them.

An element contains only one type of atom (oxygen , iron , sodium ).

A compound contains two or more types of atom chemically joined (water H2O , sodium chloride NaCl ).

A mixture contains substances that are not chemically joined (air , salt water , iron filings and sulfur ).

You can usually separate a mixture by physical methods; you need a chemical reaction to split a compound.

John Dalton (early 1800s) said: atoms are tiny, solid and indivisible; atoms of one element are identical; atoms of different elements have different masses; compounds form when atoms join in fixed ratios. Think of snooker balls of different sizes stuck together in set recipes.

Later scientists showed that atoms have a nucleus and electrons, and that atoms can be split in nuclear changes. For Year 8 you only need this contrast: Dalton’s spheres were a powerful start, but they are not the modern picture. He also did not know about protons or atomic number.

O is oxygen , Na is sodium , Fe is iron .

The second letter of a symbol is always lower case (Co is cobalt , CO is carbon monoxide — a different substance).

Formulae show the ratio of atoms: H2O is two hydrogen atoms joined to one oxygen atom .

Q. Write one idea from Dalton’s model that we still use, and one idea that later science changed.

Try this yourself first. Then tap the teacher to walk through it stage by stage.

Stage 1Ask what is joined, and how

For each substance, ask: is there only one type of atom? If yes, it is an element (even if those atoms are paired, as in O2). If there are different atoms chemically joined, it is a compound. If different substances are mixed but not joined, it is a mixture.

Stage 2Sort the list

Elements: oxygen gas (only oxygen atoms), iron (only iron atoms).

Compound: water (hydrogen and oxygen chemically joined as H2O).

Mixtures: air (nitrogen, oxygen and other gases not joined); salt water (salt mixed with water — you can get the water back by evaporation).

Stage 3What Dalton got right

We still use: substances are made of atoms; different elements have different atoms; compounds have atoms in fixed ratios (that is why formulae exist).

Stage 4What later science changed

Atoms are not solid indivisible balls. They have a nucleus and electrons. Atoms of the same element can have different masses (isotopes — stretch for later). Atomic number, not “a unique solid ball”, is what defines the element.

Final answers. Elements: O2, iron. Compound: water. Mixtures: air, salt water. Keep: atoms and fixed ratios. Change: atoms are not indivisible solid spheres.

In the real world

A sports-drink label lists “H2O, sugars, sodium chloride, flavourings”. Which of those are compounds, and which is a mixture overall? [3]

H2O, sugars and sodium chloride are compounds. The drink as a whole is a mixture of those substances (they are not chemically joined to each other).

2. How elements are organised

Must know

A group is a column . Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties — they react in similar ways and make similar types of compound. Lithium, sodium and potassium are all in Group 1, so they all react with water to make a hydroxide and hydrogen .

A period is a row. As you move left to right across a period, you gradually change from metals towards non-metals. Sodium (metal) and chlorine (non-metal) are in the same period (period 3) but different groups, so their chemistry is very different.

A staircase / zigzag line separates metals from non-metals . Hydrogen is a non-metal even though it is shown at the top left . Metalloids (such as silicon) sit near the staircase.

Group 1 — alkali metals . Group 7 — halogens (sometimes labelled Group 17) . Group 0 (or Group 8) — noble gases . The wide block in the middle is the transition metals (iron, copper, gold).

Atomic number is the number of protons. That number decides which element you have. The table is not a random poster — it is a map: group for similar reactions, period for the gradual metal-to-non-metal change, atomic number for the order.

Worked example

Q. Sodium is in Group 1 , period 3. Chlorine is in Group 7 , period 3. Argon is in Group 0 , period 3. What does each fact tell you, and why is hydrogen’s position awkward?

Try this yourself first. Then tap the teacher to walk through it stage by stage.

Stage 1Same period, different groups

Find period 3 first: that is the third row down, reading across from left to right. Sodium (Na), chlorine (Cl) and argon (Ar) are all in period 3 , so they sit on the same row. A period is like a floor in a building: sharing a floor only tells you they are at the same height on the table, not that they behave the same.

Across any period the elements change. You start on the left with metals and move towards non-metals on the right. So period 3 is a journey, not a family. Sodium is still a metal; chlorine is a reactive non-metal; argon is an unreactive gas. Same row, very different substances.

The fact that tells you about chemistry is the group (the column). Group 1 elements react in similar ways to each other; Group 7 elements do too; Group 0 barely react at all. Sodium, chlorine and argon are in three different groups, so you should expect three different kinds of reaction, even though they share a period.

So when a question gives both group and period, split the jobs: period = which row / how far the element is across the metal-to-non-metal change; group = which family it belongs to and how it is likely to react.

Stage 2Read each group

Sodium (Group 1 ) is a reactive metal: expect alkali-metal behaviour with water. Chlorine (Group 7 ) is a reactive non-metal halogen. Argon (Group 0 ) is a noble gas: almost no reactions. Across the period you have gone metal → non-metal → unreactive gas.

Stage 3Hydrogen

Hydrogen is a non-metal gas, but it is often shown above Group 1 because it has some similarities with alkali metals in later (GCSE) models. At Year 8, say: hydrogen is a non-metal in an awkward place, not an alkali metal.

Final answers. Sodium, chlorine and argon are all in period 3 — they share the same row. That only tells you where they sit on the table. It does not mean they react in the same way, because a period is a metal-to-non-metal change, not a chemical family.

The chemistry comes from the group (the column). Sodium is Group 1 , so it is an alkali metal and you expect it to react with water. Chlorine is Group 7 , so it is a halogen (a reactive non-metal). Argon is Group 0 , so it is a noble gas and almost does not react.

Hydrogen’s position is awkward because it is shown at the top left, above Group 1, but it is not an alkali metal. It is a non-metal gas. At Year 8, say: hydrogen is in an odd place on the poster; do not treat it as Group 1.

Common mistakes (Racing a child and pretending to let them win badly.)

  • Mixing up group (column) and period (row).
  • Thinking hydrogen is an alkali metal.

Group vs period. Memory hook: a period in a sentence goes across the page; a period on the table also goes across. Groups go down, like a group of friends standing in a line.

In the real world

A jeweller needs a metal that does not react easily with air or water. Using only the idea of the periodic table, why is gold a better choice than potassium? [2]

Potassium is a Group 1 alkali metal (very reactive). Gold is a transition metal, much less reactive, so it stays shiny and does not react with water or air in everyday use.

3. Physical properties and physical trends

Must know

Things you can measure or observe without making a new substance are physical properties — for example melting point, boiling point, density, hardness, colour, and whether it conducts electricity. A chemical property is about how something reacts — does it burn? Does it fizz in water?

Metals are usually shiny, can be bent or hammered into shape (malleable), ring when you hit them (sonorous), and conduct heat and electricity well . Non-metals are usually dull, brittle if solid, and do not conduct electricity (graphite is a famous exception) . Learn the general pattern — there are always a few odd ones out.

A trend is a pattern you spot as you go down a group. For example, melting points in Group 1 go: Li 181 °C → Na 98 °C → K 63 °C → Rb 39 °C — each one is lower. Once you spot the pattern, you can predict what the next element will be like.

The alkali metals are soft and shiny when you cut them. They are stored under oil to stop them reacting with air. As you go down Group 1, the melting point gets lower. Lithium melts at 181 °C but caesium melts at only 28 °C. Density mostly goes up — but potassium’s density is lower than sodium’s, a handy anomaly to remember.

As you go down Group 7, melting and boiling points get higher. That is why fluorine and chlorine are gases, bromine is a liquid, and iodine is a solid at room temperature. The colours also get darker: pale yellow → green → red-brown → dark grey. Noble gases (Group 0) follow the same idea — boiling points rise going down, but they are all still gases at room temperature.

Try this yourself first. Then tap the teacher to walk through it stage by stage.

Stage 1Look down the group, not across

Li, Na, K, Rb are Group 1 in order. The melting points 181 → 98 → 63 → 39 are getting smaller. Trend: melting point decreases down Group 1.

Stage 2Predict caesium

Caesium is below rubidium, so its melting point should be lower than 39 °C. A sensible estimate is around 20–30 °C (actual is about 28 °C). Do not invent a number higher than rubidium — that would break the trend.

Stage 3Halogen states

The opposite pattern: boiling points increase down Group 7, so the elements change gas → gas → liquid → solid. F2 gas, Cl2 gas, Br2 liquid, I2 solid.

Final answers. Group 1 melting point decreases; Cs below 39 °C (about 28 °C). Halogens: gas, gas, liquid, solid.

Density is physical. How vigorously something reacts is chemical. Potassium can have a surprising density and still be more reactive than sodium.

Iodine is a solid non-metal. Carbon (graphite/diamond) is a solid non-metal too. State depends on melting/boiling point, not on “non-metal = gas”.

In the real world

Chlorine is used to kill microbes in swimming pools. Why is it practical that chlorine is a gas at room temperature, while iodine (used in some antiseptics) is a solid? [2]

Chlorine can be dissolved from a gas into water and spread through a pool. Iodine as a solid is easier to store and put into creams or solutions in small amounts. The different states follow the Group 7 boiling-point trend.
